Slide 10 — Our implementation (9:20–10:05) — Yueqi Lin

First, the notation we use from here on. T is the number of months in the training window: twelve, sixty or one hundred twenty. L is the number of random features the model uses, from 2 up to 7,000. And c equals L over T, the number of features per observation. That is the complexity.

We use the same 159 factors and the same two signal sets as the paper. Study A uses the Welch–Goyal macro variables. Study B uses the momentum signals.

For the random Fourier features, we use 100 feature draws at T equal to twelve, as in the paper. At sixty and one hundred twenty we use ten draws, to save time; the paper uses 100 there too.

What we cut: L is capped at 7,000, where the paper goes up to 12,000.

Slide 11 — Figures (10:05–11:20)  [notebook section 3]

Let's now compare the main results against the paper. On top is the paper's out-of-sample average Sharpe Ratio, panels A and B, for T equal to twelve. Panel A is Welch–Goyal, B is momentum. Notice the broken axis: the far right is c near a thousand, so horizontal distance is not uniform. Below are our versions on the same axes: c is linear up to 50, then a break, and the small panel at the right is our largest model, c equal to 500, where the paper shows 1,000. The colour code is the same. Each line is a different ridge penalty z.

Three things match. With large ridge penalty, Sharpe rises steeply and then flattens. With small z, there is a dip right at c equal to one. And the large-z curves lie on top of each other, which is the scale invariance from earlier. But keep that last point in mind: in the paper the large-z curves do not all coincide. We come back to it in the backup slide on feature scaling.

Slide 12 — R² and coefficient norm (11:20–12:05)  [notebook section 3]

Let's now look at metrics other than Sharpe. On the left is the paper's Figure 1, on the right is ours, in the same layout: a row per study, R squared in the first column and the size of the coefficient vector in the second. R squared is negative here, which is normal for return prediction. What matters is the shape. With small z, R squared collapses near c equal to one and the coefficient norm spikes, because the model fits the training window exactly. For momentum, ours reaches minus 1.67. Past c of one, both recover. One mismatch: the paper's norm keeps falling toward zero, while ours levels off at about 7.8. We come back to that on slide 13.

Slide 13 — Expected return and volatility (12:05–12:50)  [notebook section 3]

Next, the two pieces of the Sharpe ratio. Figure 2 shows the strategy's expected return and its volatility, with the paper on the left and ours on the right. Sharpe is roughly return divided by volatility. At small z, expected return stays near 0.04 per month as c grows, while volatility falls from a spike at c equal to one. So the Sharpe rise comes from lower risk, not from higher return. That fits the shrinkage story: the position gets smoother and smaller. At large z, both go toward zero in ours, while the paper's curves for z of 10 and 100 stay higher. That is the feature scaling again, which is in a backup slide.

Slide 14 — Replication scorecard (12:50–13:50)  [notebook sections 3 and 4]

This is the scorecard for Sharpe at large z, paper against ours, for each training window. At T equal to twelve, Welch–Goyal goes from 0.117 to 0.368 in ours, a factor of 3.1, against about 0.11 to 0.36 in the paper. Momentum goes from 0.166 to 0.342, a factor of 2.1, against about 0.10 to 0.30. Ninety-six to ninety-eight percent of factors improve.

At sixty and one hundred twenty months the gain shrinks a lot: Welch–Goyal goes from 0.266 to 0.335 at sixty and from 0.296 to 0.326 at one hundred twenty, and momentum from 0.277 to 0.345 and from 0.345 to 0.363. That is about 1.25 times at sixty and 1.05 to 1.10 times at one hundred twenty. The paper's Figure 5 also shows a rise at these windows, in the same direction.

Slide 16 — Does complexity beat simple baselines? (14:35–15:20)

Now a question the paper does not ask. Its panels compare small and large models within the same family. They do not include an always-long benchmark.

So we added it. At T equal to twelve the model gets 0.342. Linear ridge on the same signals gets 0.11 to 0.16. The trailing twelve-month mean gets 0.338. And simply staying long the volatility-scaled factor gets 0.353. The model clearly beats linear, matches the trailing mean, and ties always-long: minus 0.011 with a standard error of 0.017. One caveat: always-long is flattering, because the factors are signed using what the literature found worked.


Slide 17 — Placebo (15:20–16:20)

We replaced the real signals with time-shuffled versions, which destroys any predictability, and reran everything. On the left are the real signals, on the right the shuffled placebo, with one line per ridge level and the same colours as before.

At large z the rise is identical: plus 0.177 for the placebo against plus 0.176 for real signals. Real signals add roughly 0.02 on top, about four placebo standard deviations. That is detectable but small. At longer windows the real-signal edge is larger, about 0.05 to 0.07.

Our reading: most of the upward slope is a shrinkage effect. As c grows the random features converge to the kernel and the position becomes a smoother, lower-variance bet on the factor. It is not mainly learned timing.

Backup — Feature scaling: why we use 1/√L  [notebook section 5]

One implementation choice matters for how the curves look, so we want to be explicit about it. The paper divides the features by the square root of P, but it is unclear whether that means the weight count 7p, the model's width L, or something else.

We use one over the square root of L, the number of features in the model. This is the standard scaling from the random-features literature. Rahimi and Recht, and Sutherland and Schneider, normalise by the square root of the number of features, so that the inner product of two feature vectors is an average that converges to the kernel. Our scaling does exactly that for every model size, up to a constant of root two, which only shifts z by a factor of two. With it, the effective ridge does not depend on the number of features, so every curve with z of ten or more lies on the same flat line. That is the left plot.

The right plot is a check with raw features, no scaling, which we call kappa equal to one. The ridge then shrinks relative to the features as we add more, and curves for z from one to a hundred rise, peak, and fall. That looks closer to the paper's Figure 3: at c of 500, raw features give 0.33, 0.26, 0.22, 0.20, then 0.19 to 0.18, against the paper's 0.30, 0.25, 0.20, then about 0.17 to 0.15. But raw features are not kernel-consistent, the paper does not state its scaling, and we did not rerun everything with it. So we keep 1/√L as our main, and treat raw features as a candidate explanation of the mismatch, not a finding.

The key point: the best Sharpe is about 0.342 under every scaling we tried, so our conclusions about the baseline and the placebo do not depend on this choice.

- Why the placebo? (Yueqi) To separate "learned something" from "a smoother estimator has less noise". It matches the real curve, so the rise itself is not evidence of learning.
- Does the scaling change your conclusions? (Yueqi) No: the best Sharpe is about 0.342 under every scaling tried, so the no-edge-over-always-long and placebo results stand.
- Why 1/√L and not the paper's? (Yueqi) The paper's P^(-1/2) is not defined precisely. 1/√L is the kernel-consistent scaling from Rahimi–Recht and Sutherland–Schneider, applied to each model size.
