Slide 1 — Title (0:00–0:15) — Luwei Cai

Good morning. We're Team 1: Luwei Cai, Hongjun Ding, Yueqi Lin and Hao Li. Our paper is "The Virtue of Complexity Everywhere" by Kelly, Malamud and Zhou. We'll cover the idea, the model, what we found when we replicated it, and where we think it is soft. We focus on equities.


Slide 2 — The question (0:15–1:30)

The financial question is factor timing. Can we predict, out of sample, when a US equity factor will do well? Returns are noisy and histories are short. The paper's key case uses a training window of only twelve months.

The textbook answer is Occam's razor: keep the number of parameters L well below the number of observations T, or you fit noise.

The lecture gave us a different way to think about this, in Part III. There are two worlds. If your model is correctly specified, complexity is a pure statistical cost: you get classic double descent and never beat the oracle. But no return model is correctly specified. The true relationship is unknown and probably nonlinear. In that misspecified world, adding features removes approximation error, and with enough shrinkage the gain can outrun the extra noise.

The paper takes that to the data: use L much larger than T, on 159 equity factors. The claim is conditional. Sharpe rises with complexity when ridge shrinkage is large enough. It is not "bigger is always better".


Slide 3 — Setup and notation (1:30–2:30)

Here is the notation, the same as in the paper. X_t is the vector of raw signals at time t. It has d entries: fifteen Welch–Goyal macro variables in one study, two momentum signals in the other. R_{t+1} is next month's factor return, scaled by volatility known at time t.

S_t is the vector of the L random features we use, built from X_t. They come from P equals 7p random weights, each giving a sine and a cosine, so the pool has up to 2P columns. T is the number of months in the rolling training window: twelve, sixty or one hundred twenty. Complexity is c equals L over T. When c equals one, the number of features equals the number of observations, the interpolation boundary. z is the ridge penalty. And β̂ is the only thing that is learned: L weights.

Two points. Raising L adds transformations of the same d signals. It does not add any new financial data. And there are two separate studies, A with Welch–Goyal and B with momentum. They are never combined. In each, the model is estimated separately for each of the 159 factors.


Slide 4 — Data (2:30–3:00)

The universe is six Fama–French factors plus 153 JKP characteristic factors, monthly, equities only. The paper also uses futures and Treasuries; we do not.

Everything is standardised using past data only: returns by their trailing twelve-month root mean square, signals by an expanding root mean square with at least 36 months, clipped to plus or minus five.

As a data check, we recomputed mean, volatility and Sharpe for the factors and compared with the paper's Table 1. The correlations are 0.996, 0.9995 and 0.997. So we are on the same data.

Slide 5 — Model (3:00–4:30) — Hongjun Ding

Now the model. There are two parts, and it's important to be clear about which is fixed and which is learned.

The diagram at the top shows the flow, left to right. The features are fixed. For each random seed we draw P equals 7p random vectors ω_i from a standard normal in d dimensions, once, and the same ω is used for every factor at that seed. We repeat this for 100 seeds. For each, we compute the sine and the cosine of γ times ω_i transpose X_t. So each ω_i gives two features, and stacking them gives a pool of up to 2P columns, and S_t is the first L of them. We use seven bandwidths γ, from 0.1 up to 16. Smaller models are just the first L columns of the same draw. Here p is the number of weights per bandwidth, so P equals 7p. Nothing in this step is trained.

What is learned is a ridge regression on the last T months. The estimator β̂ is the matrix-inverse formula on the slide, and it is the solution to the minimisation on the right: average squared error over the window, plus z times the squared norm of β.

This is nonlinear in the signals X_t but linear in β̂. The lecture's way of saying it: a two-layer neural network with a frozen random first layer and a learned linear second layer.

One point to remember: the loss is squared error. The model does not optimise Sharpe. Sharpe is only how we evaluate it.


Slide 6 — Strategy and metrics (4:30–5:30)

Here is how a forecast becomes a trade. At date t we fit β̂ on the previous T months, all with returns already realised. The forecast π-hat at t is S_t transpose β̂, and it is also the position. The strategy earns π-hat times next month's factor return. A positive forecast means long, a negative one means short, so this is timing, not static exposure. There is no look-ahead.

We report out-of-sample R squared, the coefficient norm, expected return, volatility and annualised Sharpe. Sharpe is computed per feature draw and per factor, averaged over draws, then over the 159 factors. That is an average of individual factor-timing Sharpe ratios, not the Sharpe of a 159-factor portfolio.

One useful fact: if you multiply β̂ by a positive constant, R squared and the coefficient norm change, but Sharpe does not. We'll see why that matters later.


Slide 7 — Why L ≫ T can work (5:30–6:15)

Why doesn't this blow up? At c equal to one with almost no ridge, the model interpolates the sample. The coefficient norm and volatility spike, R squared crashes. That is the double-descent peak from the lecture.

Past c equal to one, the ridge solution is a minimum-norm fit that gets smoother with more features, and the norm falls. As c goes to infinity the random-feature model approaches kernel ridge regression. So "more complex" really means "a better-behaved smoother of the same signals", not "more overfit".

The paper's prediction at T equal to twelve follows. With small z, a dip near c equal to one and then recovery. With large z, Sharpe increasing in c. And the lecture's caveat applies: this does not license adding junk predictors.

Slide 8 — Our implementation (6:15–7:00) — Yueqi Lin

We wrote this from the paper's equations; there is no public code that we used. The ridge is solved in its dual, T by T, form, so L equal to 7,000 is cheap. We use the same 159 factors and the same two signal sets, A and B. At T equal to twelve we use 100 feature draws, as in the paper. At sixty and one hundred twenty, ten draws.

What we cut: L is capped at 7,000 rather than 12,000, and we did not replicate futures or bonds, because that data is paid. Beyond the paper we added baselines, a placebo, an exact-kernel check and a simulator.

Slide 9 — Figures (7:00–8:15)  [notebook section 4]

On top is the paper's Figure 3, panels A and B, for T equal to twelve. Panel A is Welch–Goyal, B is momentum. Notice the broken axis: the far right is c near a thousand, so horizontal distance is not uniform. Below are our versions on the same axes: c is linear up to 50, then a break, and the small panel at the right is our largest model, c equal to 500, where the paper shows 1,000. The colour code is the same. Each line is a different z.

Three things match. With large z, Sharpe rises steeply and then flattens. With small z, there is a dip right at c equal to one. And the large-z curves lie on top of each other, which is the scale invariance from earlier. But keep that last point in mind: in the paper the large-z curves do not all coincide. We come back to it on slide 11.

Slide 10 — Replication numbers (8:15–9:30)  [notebook sections 4 and 5]

In numbers, at T equal to twelve and large z. Welch–Goyal goes from 0.117 to 0.368, a factor of 3.1; the paper's curve goes from about 0.11 to 0.36. Momentum goes from 0.166 to 0.342, a factor of 2.1; the paper's from about 0.1 to 0.3. Paper values are read off the figure by eye. Ninety-six to ninety-eight percent of factors improve.

Beyond Sharpe, the paper's Figure 1 shows out-of-sample R squared. In ours it collapses to minus 1.67 at c equal to one for small z, while Sharpe stays positive, and recovers to minus 0.18; the paper's ends near minus 0.45. Expected return matches at low z, 0.039 against about 0.04. Volatility is lower in ours: 0.63 against 0.8 to 0.9.

At longer windows, the paper's Figure 5, the gain shrinks a lot: about seven hundredths at sixty months and two to three hundredths at one hundred twenty. Same direction as the paper, much smaller size. At one hundred twenty months with tiny ridge, Sharpe actually falls.

Two things did not match: the coefficient norm plateaus in ours but falls in the paper, and the large-z curves coincide in ours but fan out in the paper.

Slide 11 — Feature scaling: why we use 1/√L (9:30–10:45)  [notebook section 7]

One implementation choice matters for how the curves look, so we want to be explicit about it. The paper divides the features by the square root of P, but it is unclear whether that means the weight count 7p, the model's width L, or something else.

We use one over the square root of L, the number of features in the model. This is the standard scaling from the random-features literature. Rahimi and Recht, and Sutherland and Schneider, normalise by the square root of the number of features, so that the inner product of two feature vectors is an average that converges to the kernel. Our scaling does exactly that for every model size, up to a constant of root two, which only shifts z by a factor of two. With it, the effective ridge does not depend on the number of features, so every curve with z of ten or more lies on the same flat line. That is the left plot.

The right plot is a check with raw features, no scaling, which we call kappa equal to one. The ridge then shrinks relative to the features as we add more, and curves for z from one to a hundred rise, peak, and fall. That looks closer to the paper's Figure 3: at c of 500, raw features give 0.33, 0.26, 0.22, 0.20, then 0.19 to 0.18, against the paper's 0.30, 0.25, 0.20, then about 0.17 to 0.15. But raw features are not kernel-consistent, the paper does not state its scaling, and we did not rerun everything with it. So we keep 1/√L as our main, and treat raw features as a candidate explanation of the mismatch, not a finding.

The key point: the best Sharpe is about 0.342 under every scaling we tried, so our conclusions about the baseline and the placebo do not depend on this choice.

Slide 12 — Is the baseline fair? (10:45–11:30)

Now a question the paper does not ask. Its panels compare small and large models within the same family. They do not include an always-long benchmark.

So we added it. At T equal to twelve the model gets 0.342. Linear ridge on the same signals gets 0.11 to 0.16. The trailing twelve-month mean gets 0.338. And simply staying long the volatility-scaled factor gets 0.353. The model clearly beats linear, matches the trailing mean, and ties always-long: minus 0.011 with a standard error of 0.017. One caveat: always-long is flattering, because the factors are signed using what the literature found worked.


Slide 13 — Placebo (11:30–12:30)

We replaced the real signals with time-shuffled versions, which destroys any predictability, and reran everything. Orange is the placebo band; blue is the real signals.

At large z the rise is identical: plus 0.177 for the placebo against plus 0.176 for real signals. Real signals add roughly 0.02 on top, about four placebo standard deviations. That is detectable but small. At longer windows the real-signal edge is larger, about 0.05 to 0.07.

Our reading: most of the upward slope is a shrinkage effect. As c grows the random features converge to the kernel and the position becomes a smoother, lower-variance bet on the factor. It is not mainly learned timing.

[Handoff] "Hao will go through our critique."

Slide 14 — Critique (12:30–13:20) — Hao Li

Where is the paper vulnerable? One, most of the headline curve is shrinkage, as the placebo shows. Two, the comparison is across complexity levels, not against the obvious benchmark. Three, the factor list and signs use hindsight. Four, there are no transaction costs, on a nonlinear position refit monthly. Five, the paper does not state its feature scaling or which kernel it uses, and as Yueqi showed, that moves the ridge axis. Six, our data vintage is newer than the paper's, so small differences are expected.

Slide 15 — Takeaways (13:20–13:45)

To wrap up. Replicated: at T equal to twelve on 159 equity factors, Sharpe rises with complexity by two to three times, with the double-descent dip at small ridge. Qualified: the gain fades at longer windows and needs large ridge. Not shown: that complexity beats simply holding the factor, or that the rise is learned timing.

The lesson from implementing it: the shape of the curve is robust, but its economic value is not established. Code, data and every number are in our repository. We're happy to take questions.
