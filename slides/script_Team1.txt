Slide 1 — Title (0:00–0:15) — Luwei Cai

Good morning. We're Team 1: Luwei Cai, Hongjun Ding, Yueqi Lin and Hao Li. Our paper is "The Virtue of Complexity Everywhere" by Kelly, Malamud and Zhou. We'll cover the idea, the model, what we found when we replicated it, and where we think it is soft. We focus on equities.


Slide 2 — The question (0:15–1:30)

The financial question is factor timing. Can we predict, out of sample, when a US equity factor will do well? Returns are noisy and histories are short. The paper's key case uses a training window of only twelve months.

The textbook answer is Occam's razor: keep the number of parameters L well below the number of observations T, or you fit noise.

The lecture gave us a different way to think about this, in Part III. There are two worlds. If your model is correctly specified, complexity is a pure statistical cost: you get classic double descent and never beat the oracle. But no return model is correctly specified. The true relationship is unknown and probably nonlinear. In that misspecified world, adding features removes approximation error, and with enough shrinkage the gain can outrun the extra noise.

The paper takes that to the data: use L much larger than T, on 159 equity factors. The claim is conditional. Sharpe rises with complexity when ridge shrinkage is large enough. It is not "bigger is always better".


Slide 3 — Setup and notation (1:30–2:30)

Here is the notation, the same as in the paper. X_t is the vector of raw signals at time t. It has d entries: fifteen Welch–Goyal macro variables in one study, two momentum signals in the other. R_{t+1} is next month's factor return, scaled by volatility known at time t.

S_t is the vector of the L random features we use, built from X_t. They come from P equals 7p random weights, each giving a sine and a cosine, so the pool has up to 2P columns. T is the number of months in the rolling training window: twelve, sixty or one hundred twenty. Complexity is c equals L over T. When c equals one, the number of features equals the number of observations, the interpolation boundary. z is the ridge penalty. And β̂ is the only thing that is learned: L weights.

Two points. Raising L adds transformations of the same d signals. It does not add any new financial data. And there are two separate studies, A with Welch–Goyal and B with momentum. They are never combined. In each, the model is estimated separately for each of the 159 factors.


Slide 4 — Data (2:30–3:00)

The universe is six Fama–French factors plus 153 JKP characteristic factors, monthly, equities only. The paper also uses futures and Treasuries; we do not.

Everything is standardised using past data only: returns by their trailing twelve-month root mean square, signals by an expanding root mean square with at least 36 months, clipped to plus or minus five.

As a data check, we recomputed mean, volatility and Sharpe for the factors and compared with the paper's Table 1. The correlations are 0.996, 0.9995 and 0.997. So we are on the same data.

Slide 5 — The method (3:00–3:45) — Hongjun Ding

Now the method. The paper assumes that the next return is a function of the information we have today, plus noise: R at t plus one equals f of X t, plus an error. X t is the information available at the forecast date, and R at t plus one is the next volatility-scaled return. We do not know f, so the paper builds the forecast in three steps: raw signals, then nonlinear features, then a ridge forecast.

Slide 6 — Inputs and prediction targets (3:45–4:35)

This table shows what goes in and what we predict. For equity factors, the input is either fifteen Welch–Goyal macro variables or two momentum signals, last month's return and the average of the eleven months before it, and the target is the factor's next-month return. The paper applies the same method to futures and to US Treasuries, with their own signals and targets. We focus on the equity factors. In every case, the inputs and the returns are scaled using historical volatility, so everything is on a comparable footing.

Slide 7 — From raw signals to random features (4:35–5:50)

Here is how the raw signals become features. Each random projection gives one sine and one cosine. We draw a Gaussian vector w, take its product with the signal X t, scale it by one of seven constants a, and apply sine and cosine. The seven constants set the frequency scales. These weights are drawn once and then fixed: nothing in this step is trained. Increasing L just makes the feature vector S t wider. Whatever the width, the model outputs one return forecast.

Slide 8 — Fit the output weights with ridge (5:50–6:50)

What is learned is the output layer. We choose the weights beta to minimise the average squared forecast error over the last T training pairs, plus z times the squared norm of beta. That is a mean squared error term and a ridge penalty. In each run, the random feature map and the ridge penalty z are fixed. In each window, the L output weights are refitted, one per feature. A positive z gives a unique fit even when L exceeds T. Note that the loss is squared error: the model does not optimise Sharpe.

Slide 9 — Turn forecasts into positions (6:50–7:40)

Now we turn forecasts into positions. At each date we fit on the past T pairs, where the next return is already known. We score today's signals with the feature vector S t. The forecast is beta transpose S t, and it is also the position: its size and direction are in volatility-scaled units. Then the next return is realised, and the strategy earns the position times that return. We advance the window by one month and refit. There is no look-ahead.

Slide 10 — Vary complexity in a controlled test (7:40–8:50)

The paper varies complexity in a controlled way. Complexity c is L over T, the number of features per training observation. When c equals one, the number of features equals the number of observations: the interpolation boundary. In the paper, the feature count L goes from 2 to 12,000, the training window T is 12, 60 or 120, the ridge penalty z takes seven values from ten to the minus three to ten to the three, and the random features are drawn with 100 seeds. At each width we use the first L features, so wider models keep the earlier features. We evaluate the forecasts with out-of-sample R squared and the positions with Sharpe.

[Handoff] "Hao will take you through the paper's own results."

Slide 11 — Larger models improve Sharpe with enough ridge (8:50–10:05) — Hao Li

These are the paper's equity results from Figure Three, with twelve months in each training window. The left panel uses fifteen macroeconomic predictors; the right uses two momentum signals. Across each panel, complexity increases as the model uses more random features. The vertical axis is average out-of-sample Sharpe, and colors represent ridge penalties.

Weak shrinkage produces a dip near complexity one, where features equal training observations. With sufficient shrinkage, Sharpe rises as models grow. The authors describe roughly a threefold improvement from low to high complexity. The original financial information stays fixed: larger models get more transformations, not more data. This supports greater flexibility under appropriate regularization.

These scores average separate strategies across one hundred and fifty-nine factors; they are not the Sharpe of a single combined portfolio. The broader paper also reports evidence for futures and bonds.

Slide 12 — Prediction accuracy improves with appropriate ridge (10:05–10:40)

This panel from Figure One measures prediction accuracy directly, using the macroeconomic inputs and twelve-month window. Zero means matching the sample-mean forecast; positive values mean lower squared forecast error. With weak ridge, accuracy collapses near complexity one and then recovers. The bottom of that dip is outside the displayed range. Appropriate ridge produces small positive scores. So Sharpe and R-squared measure different outcomes, and the paper examines both.

Slide 13 — The pattern also appears with longer windows (10:40–11:20)

Figure Five asks whether the Sharpe pattern depends on training with only twelve months. The left column uses sixty months and the right uses one hundred and twenty. The top row uses macroeconomic predictors; the bottom uses momentum signals. With sufficient shrinkage, the authors again report gains from complexity, although many curves level off quickly.

The comparison is larger versus smaller models within the same family. It does not establish superiority over simple financial alternatives. Our implementation section examines that distinction.

Slide 14 — Our implementation (11:20–12:05) — Yueqi Lin

First, I want to make clear the parameters we use here. T is the number of months in the training window: twelve, sixty or one hundred twenty. This part is consistent with the paper. L is the number of random features the model uses, from 2 up to 7,000. Note that to reduce run time, we have capped L at 7,000, where the paper goes up to 12,000. We don't expect this to introduce much discrepancies in the results because we saw the performance stabilizing at 7000.  
And c is the complexity, which equals L over T, representing the number of features per observation window. 

We use the same 159 factors and the same two signal sets as the paper. Study A uses the Welch–Goyal macro variables. Study B uses the momentum signals.

For the random Fourier features, we use 100 feature draws at T equal to twelve, as in the paper. At sixty and one hundred twenty we use ten draws, to save time; the paper uses 100 there too. 


Slide 15 — OOS Sharpe vs complexity, paper and ours (12:05–13:20)  [notebook section 3]

Let's now compare the main results against the paper. On top is the paper's out-of-sample average Sharpe Ratio, Study A and B, for T equal to twelve. Below are our versions on the same axes. 

The shape generally matches. With small ridge penalty, it first increases and then there is a dip right at c equal to one, then rises steadily, which exhibits a double ascent shape. With large ridge penalty, Sharpe rises steeply and then flattens. The double ascent turns into a permanent ascent. In our replication, the large-z curves lie on top of each other. In the paper the large-z curves do not all coincide. We think this is simply to do with a scaling factor in the random fourier features.


Slide 16 — Expected return and volatility (13:20–14:05)  [notebook section 3]

Next is expected return and volatility, again with the paper on the left and ours on the right. At small ridge penalty, expected return stays near 0.04 per month as c grows, while volatility falls from a spike at c equal to one. So past c equal to one, the Sharpe rise comes from lower volatility, not higher return. The position gets smoother and smaller as complexity increases. At large z, both volatility and expected return are lower in ours, while the paper's stay higher. We think that is to do with feature scaling again.


Slide 17 — Sharpe at large z, paper vs ours (14:05–15:05)  [notebook sections 3 and 4]

This is the comparison table for Sharpe as we increase to large ridge penalty z, for each training window. At T equal to twelve, our Welch–Goyal increased a by factor of 3.1, against about 3 times in the paper, which is consistent. However, our momentum increased by a factor of 2.1, against also about 3 times in the paper. 

At sixty and one hundred twenty months the gain shrinks a lot: Welch–Goyal increased by 1.26 times at sixty and 1.1 times at one hundred twenty, and momentum 1.25 times and 1.05 times. We see the effect deteriorating at longer training windows. This is consistent with the paper's finding that complex models perform particularly well when estimated over extremely short windows. 

[The reason is that complex models leverage high dimensionality of the signal space in the same way classic models leverage long time series.]

Slide 18 — Complexity gains do not establish a timing edge (15:05–16:15) — Hao Li

My first critique concerns the benchmark. The paper's main figures compare model sizes, but do not include an always-long factor strategy. Our equity replication adds that comparison. With twelve-month training and strong ridge, momentum inputs give a mean annualized Sharpe of zero point three four two, versus zero point three five three for always long. Macroeconomic inputs give zero point three six eight, versus zero point three four nine.

Those small gaps do not establish a reliable timing advantage. Always long is an informative comparison, although the factors' published directions make it imperfect. This qualifies the economic interpretation; it does not overturn the larger-versus-smaller-model finding.

A fair test therefore needs the same evaluation dates and risk scaling for each comparison. We also need uncertainty estimates that allow different factor strategies to move together.

Slide 19 — A usable strategy needs further tests (16:15–17:05)

My second critique concerns practical use. Trading must survive turnover, financing and transaction costs. These curves do not provide that net-performance test.

A usable strategy also needs a rule for choosing the ridge penalty using only past data. Picking the best curve after seeing test results would exaggerate performance. This is a follow-up test, not a claim of bias.

One practical next step is to choose the penalty on an earlier validation period, freeze it, and then evaluate a later period after realistic costs.

Complexity gains are supported within this model family. Profitability after costs remains unproven.

Likely questions, with the best person to answer

- Why does Sharpe not depend on z at large z? (Hongjun) Sharpe is invariant to a positive rescaling of the position, so when z is large only the direction of β̂ matters. Backup B.
- Is there look-ahead? (Hongjun) Signals and return scaling use past data only, and the ridge is fit on a window ending at t. Factor signs and the factor list do use hindsight (backup slide on feature scaling).
- Why not just MSE or R² as the criterion? (Hongjun) R² is negative at large L in our results while Sharpe rises: squared error punishes forecast scale, Sharpe does not. Backup A and B.
- Why the placebo? (Yueqi) To separate "learned something" from "a smoother estimator has less noise". It matches the real curve, so the rise itself is not evidence of learning.
- Does the scaling change your conclusions? (Yueqi) No: the best Sharpe is about 0.342 under every scaling tried, so the no-edge-over-always-long and placebo results stand.
- Why 1/√L and not the paper's? (Yueqi) The paper's P^(-1/2) is not defined precisely. 1/√L is the kernel-consistent scaling from Rahimi–Recht and Sutherland–Schneider, applied to each model size.
- Would more draws change the T = 60 results? (Yueqi) We checked with 100 draws, as in the paper. The high end did not move (0.345 for momentum, 0.335 for Welch–Goyal); only the smallest models moved, by about 0.02 to 0.03, which changes the size of the rise. Baseline, placebo and scaling conclusions do not change. The 100-draw results are in the repo.
- Why not bonds or futures? (Yueqi) Out of scope: the paper's data are paid or not available. We cover US equity factors only.

- Why is P in the lecture called L in the paper? (Luwei) Notation. The paper's L is the number of features used, which the lecture calls P. We use the paper's. In the paper P = 7p is the number of random weights, and each gives two features.
- What is 7p? (Hongjun) p is the number of random weights per bandwidth, and there are 7 bandwidths, so P = 7p weights. Each weight gives a sin and a cos feature, so the pool has 2P columns. We use p = 500, so 3,500 weights and 7,000 columns.

Backup — Baselines: does complexity beat simple baselines? (deck p. 21)

Now a question the paper does not ask. Its panels compare small and large models within the same family. They do not include an always-long benchmark.

So we added it. At T equal to twelve the model gets 0.342. Linear ridge on the same signals gets 0.11 to 0.16. And simply staying long the volatility-scaled factor gets 0.353. The model clearly beats linear, and ties always-long: minus 0.011 with a standard error of 0.017. One caveat: always-long is flattering, because the factors are signed using what the literature found worked.

Backup — Placebo with time-shuffled signals (deck p. 22)

We replaced the real signals with time-shuffled versions, which destroys any predictability, and reran everything. The solid lines are the real signals and the dashed lines the shuffled placebo, with the same colour for each ridge level.

The shapes are identical: most of the upward slope is a shrinkage effect. As c grows the random features converge to the kernel and the position becomes a smoother, lower-variance bet on the factor. It is not mainly learned timing.

At large z, real signals add roughly 0.02 on top. That is detectable but small. At longer windows the real-signal edge is larger, about 0.05 to 0.07.

Backup — Critique (earlier version) (deck p. 23)

Where is the paper vulnerable? One, most of the headline curve is shrinkage, as the placebo shows. Two, the comparison is across complexity levels, not against the obvious benchmark. Three, the factor list and signs use hindsight. Four, there are no transaction costs, on a nonlinear position refit monthly. Five, the paper does not state its feature scaling or which kernel it uses, and as Yueqi showed, that moves the ridge axis. Six, our data vintage is newer than the paper's, so small differences are expected.

Backup — Takeaways (deck p. 24)

To wrap up. Replicated: at T equal to twelve on 159 equity factors, Sharpe rises with complexity by two to three times, with the double-descent dip at small ridge. Qualified: the gain fades at longer windows and needs large ridge. Not shown: that complexity beats simply holding the factor, or that the rise is learned timing.

The lesson from implementing it: the shape of the curve is robust, but its economic value is not established. Code, data and every number are in our repository. We're happy to take questions.

Backup — Why squared error and Sharpe can disagree (deck p. 25)

Mean squared error is E[R²] − 2E[π̂R] + E[π̂²]: realised-return variation, a timing term, and forecast magnitude. A forecast with the right direction but wrong scale has poor R² and a good Sharpe, and scaling π̂ by a positive constant leaves the Sharpe of π̂_t R_{t+1} unchanged.

Backup — Feature scaling: why we use 1/√L (deck p. 28)  [notebook section 5]

One implementation choice matters for how the curves look, so we want to be explicit about it. The paper divides the features by the square root of P, but it is unclear whether that means the weight count 7p, the model's width L, or something else.

We use one over the square root of L, the number of features in the model. This is the standard scaling from the random-features literature. Rahimi and Recht, and Sutherland and Schneider, normalise by the square root of the number of features, so that the inner product of two feature vectors is an average that converges to the kernel. Our scaling does exactly that for every model size, up to a constant of root two, which only shifts z by a factor of two. With it, the effective ridge does not depend on the number of features, so every curve with z of ten or more lies on the same flat line. That is the left plot.

The right plot is a check with raw features, no scaling, which we call kappa equal to one. The ridge then shrinks relative to the features as we add more, and curves for z from one to a hundred rise, peak, and fall. That looks closer to the paper's Figure 3: at c of 500, raw features give 0.33, 0.26, 0.22, 0.20, then 0.19 to 0.18, against the paper's 0.30, 0.25, 0.20, then about 0.17 to 0.15. But raw features are not kernel-consistent, the paper does not state its scaling, and we did not rerun everything with it. So we keep 1/√L as our main, and treat raw features as a candidate explanation of the mismatch, not a finding.

The key point: the best Sharpe is about 0.342 under every scaling we tried, so our conclusions about the baseline and the placebo do not depend on this choice.
